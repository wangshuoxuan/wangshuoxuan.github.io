  我对播放器上的数字有些挑剔。视频常调到三倍，音频多在2.1到2.25倍之间；能以0.05倍为单位微调，当然更好。有时，恰恰是2.15倍，听起来最舒服。图书馆里、吃饭时、独自走路或做简单家务的时候，我都会听点什么。除非正在和人说话，或刻意让头脑休息，我通常总能找到一段值得打开的内容。

  ## 一｜我怎样开始习惯两倍以上的速度

  高中准备物理竞赛时，我主要靠书本自学，偶尔用1.5倍速听音频。大一仍以阅读为主。我在微信读书上读了很多电子书，也试过在喜马拉雅上听完整本书，却常觉得不如自己读来得合适：文字可以跳读、回看，在难处停一停，查点资料，或者换一本书；听书则只能顺着讲述的次序往下走。后来我接触到得到、樊登讲书，以及主题更集中的课程。一份讲书可能有二三十分钟，加速后大约十分钟就能了解轮廓。有时一天听过十本左右的解读，再挑一两本找原书。这不等于读完原书，却让我有机会碰见更多陌生的思想。大学里更多时间需要自己安排；一个人吃饭，或在楼与楼之间走一段路，都可以顺手打开一节课。

  ## 二｜2.15倍速里，我究竟在做什么

  太慢，我会无聊；太快，可能每个字都听见了，意思却来不及连起来。舒服的速度处在两者之间。这是我的使用感受，不是经实验测出的最优倍率；它随讲者、内容密度和已有知识而变化。熟悉的人文社科讲解、访谈和对谈，我更愿意加速；但一段难懂的论证，不会因为属于文科就变得容易。一张电路图，也可能要看上几分钟。我不会为每个漏听的细节都回放，但课堂上忽然笑成一片，我却没听清老师刚才说了什么，这时我一定会倒回十几秒，调回原速，甚至降到0.75倍。复杂课程第一遍听完似乎明白，隔几天再看课件却讲不清楚。后来重新看文字、做题或把推理过程走一遍，才慢慢接上。熟悉不等于掌握。

  ## 三｜先浓缩内容，再加快播放

  一段讲书，在我碰到倍速按钮之前，就已经替我加速了一次。讲述者挑出主线，重新组织材料，把有力量的例子放到显眼的位置。我再把这份经过编辑的内容调快。假设一本完整有声书要听十小时，讲书把它浓缩成三十分钟，三倍速再把三十分钟变成十分钟。前一次删减和重组内容，后一次加快剩余内容的到达；时间缩短了，却不意味着十小时的理解可以原封不动地装进十分钟。我记得讲书里关于塞万提斯与勒班陀海战的故事，也记得把史汀生到访京都与他后来反对轰炸京都联系起来的讲述。它们让我感到，一段私人经历可能进入更大的历史因果链；当然，真正的历史决策远比一个故事复杂。读《克里米亚战争史》时，我也发现，浓缩解读里被略去的等待、消耗和外交博弈，恰恰构成了历史的大部分。

  ## 四｜趁兴趣还在，走进一个世界

  我听自己感兴趣的艺术课程时，会一口气听很久。一个主题牵出下一个主题，越听越想知道后面还有什么；从早上听到晚上，第二天醒来还想继续。那时我不是催促自己完成任务，而是不舍得让刚被打开的世界中断。陈文捷讲罗马建筑时，我仿佛置身城市上空，看那些建筑沉积着不同时代的痕迹。建筑连着文明的起源、延绵的文脉，也连着无数曾在其间生活的人。我希望图片停得久一点，让自己顺着讲述，把这些联系一点点想出来。这时候，我完全不想加速。此刻能够在想象中抵达那里，已经是我想要的。

  ## 五｜省下的时间，去了哪里

  一段三小时的内容，一小时就听完，我很少把另外两小时空着，通常会再开一门课。这让我想到杰文斯讨论燃料效率时提出的机制：资源使用更有效率，需求也可能随之增加。我的听课只是一个类比，但那个方向很熟悉：每门课花的时间少了，总听课时间反而增加。我常用内容数量和原始时长衡量泛听进度，知道它们只是替代指标：听完的数量看得见，判断力的变化看不见。B站收藏夹和微信读书书架都曾增长得很快；收藏令人愉快，因为那一刻，我已经能想象学完之后的自己。真正开始学，却可能要经过很长一段不理解的过程。我想在通识上见多识广，也想在专业上钻得足够深，这两种愿望却不会自动变成同一种时间安排。

  ## 六｜当效率开始要求别人

  从中学起，就有人说我讲话太快。我曾觉得，快速而清楚地表达，是替别人节约时间。可有时我说了一大段，对方的第一句回应却是：“你刚才说什么？”如果还得从头再来，双方的时间都没有省下。语音里，妈妈有时刚开了个头，我就觉得后面几句话已经猜到了。我会不耐烦，打断她，催她换个话题。挂断之后，又觉得自己本可以温和一点。父母花了很多年把我养大，我如今在北京，一学期回家一两次，却还在把几句熟悉的叮嘱，当成一场效率不高的信息传递。我不喜欢把一个人当成简历来读；认真交谈时，我会把手机放远，留意对方的表情、停顿和语调。一旦进入真正的深谈，我不愿破坏它的节奏。

  ## 七｜十六倍速的《红楼梦》

  高中时，我听到过把《红楼梦》当作考试材料的讨论：人物关系是否了解，某个情节是否知道，题目来了能不能答。有人建议读不下原著的同学通过电视剧熟悉内容；也听说有人继续加速，甚至调到十六倍。如果考试主要奖励“知道发生了什么”，这种方法对一个很窄的目标确实高效。但它不等于充分体验作品。一个停顿、演员的神情、一场戏的气息，以及人物感受一点点发生的变化，对情节题可能帮助有限，却可能构成这场戏最值得看的部分。知道发生了什么，不等于真正经历了它。目标不同，合理的速度也会不同。

  ## 八｜给自己的想法留一点空地

  有时手机没电，或者忘了带，我走在校园里就什么也不听。起初只是觉得耳边安静下来，后来会发现，脑子并没有因此停下来。我会想起最近遇到的问题，想到一半又联想到别的事情；也会看看树、建筑和来来往往的人。后来我知道，心理学和脑科学把一部分与自发思考、回忆个人经历、想象未来有关的活动，同默认模式网络（Default Mode Network，DMN）联系起来。这个名字让我有了一个理解自己体验的角度：当注意力暂时不再追着外界的新信息跑，头脑仍然可以整理已有的记忆、感受和念头，把看似不相干的东西连在一起。对我来说，散步和短暂放空，是给这些联系一点自然形成的空间。所以我想保留一点不听、不看、也不急着完成什么的时间。它不必每次都带来灵感；有时候只是放松，有时候想明白一件事，有时候什么也没有发生，这都没关系。

I am particular about the numbers on a media player. Video often runs at 3×; audio usually sits between 2.1× and 2.25×. If I can adjust in increments of 0.05, all the better. Sometimes 2.15× is simply the pace that feels right. I listen in the library, over meals, on walks, and while doing simple chores. Unless I am talking to someone or deliberately letting my mind rest, I can usually find something to play.

## 1. How I got used to going beyond 2×

In high school, I prepared for physics competitions mainly through books and occasionally listened to audio at around 1.5×. I still read a great deal in my first year at university. I tried listening to entire audiobooks on Ximalaya, but reading often worked better: I could skim, reread, stop to look something up, or abandon a book. Audio kept me moving through the narrator’s sequence. Later, I found more focused book talks and lectures on Dedao and other platforms. A twenty- or thirty-minute explanation could give me a first map of a subject in about ten minutes. Sometimes I listened to around ten summaries in a day, then chose one or two books to read more carefully. A summary is not the original, but it can show me where I might want to go next. University gave me more control over my time; a meal alone or a walk between buildings could become another listening session.

## 2. What am I doing at 2.15×?

Too slow, and I get bored. Too fast, and I hear the words without keeping up with the thought. The useful range lies between them. This is my experience, not a scientifically measured optimum. It depends on the speaker, the density of the material, and what I already know. Familiar humanities lectures and conversational interviews can move quickly for me; a difficult argument or an engineering diagram may need much more time.

I do not rewind every missed detail. But if a class suddenly bursts into laughter and I have missed the line that caused it, I go back ten or fifteen seconds, sometimes even to 0.75×. Complex courses can feel clear on a fast first pass and become confusing when I return to the slides days later. Reviewing the text, solving a problem, or rebuilding the argument is what makes the understanding stick. Familiarity is not mastery.

## 3. Compressing the content before speeding it up

A book talk has already accelerated the material before I touch the speed control. The speaker selects the main thread, rearranges the argument, and brings the strongest examples forward. I then speed up that edited account. A ten-hour audiobook might become a thirty-minute summary, and 3× playback turns those thirty minutes into ten. The first operation removes and reorganizes content; the second makes what remains arrive faster. Neither means ten hours of understanding can be packed intact into ten minutes.

I remember a story about Cervantes and the Battle of Lepanto, and another that connected Henry Stimson’s visit to Kyoto with his later opposition to making the city an atomic-bomb target. Both made me think about how a personal experience might enter a much larger chain of history, though real historical decisions are more complicated than a neat anecdote. After hearing a condensed account of the Crimean War, I read more and found long stretches of waiting, attrition, and diplomacy. Summaries take me to the dramatic moments, but not through the whole history.

## 4. Following an interest while it is alive

When an art course catches my attention, one topic leads to another. I can listen from morning into the evening and want to continue the next day. I am not forcing myself through a checklist; I do not want to interrupt a world that has just opened up.

Chen Wenjie’s lecture on Rome and ancient Western architecture gave me a different experience. I imagined looking down on the city, seeing buildings layered with traces of different ages. Architecture connected me to the origins of a civilization, its cultural memory, and the lives that had accumulated there. I wanted to look at the images slowly and let the connections form. I did not want to speed up. Being there in thought was already the point.

## 5. Where the saved time goes

If I finish a three-hour recording in one hour, I rarely leave the other two hours empty. I open another course. This reminds me of Jevons’s discussion of efficiency: using a resource more efficiently can increase demand for it. My listening is only an analogy, but the pattern feels familiar. Each lecture takes less time, while my total listening time grows.

For exploratory listening, I often measure progress by quantity and original duration. I know these are only proxies: completed items are visible, changes in judgment are not. My Bilibili watchlist and WeRead bookshelf grew quickly because saving a course or book lets me imagine the person I might become after finishing it. Actually learning can involve a long, awkward stretch of not understanding. I want breadth across fields and depth in my own work, but those ambitions do not automatically call for the same schedule.

## 6. When efficiency starts to govern relationships

People have told me since school that I speak too quickly. I once thought fast, clear speech was a courtesy to the listener. But if I finish a long explanation and hear, “What did you just say?”, neither of us has saved time.

On voice calls, I sometimes predict what my mother is about to say and become impatient, interrupting her or asking her to move on. Afterwards I wish I had been gentler. My parents spent years raising me; now I live in Beijing and return home only once or twice a term, yet I can treat familiar reminders as inefficient information. I do not like conversations that feel like reading a résumé. When I care about a conversation, I put my phone away and pay attention to expressions, pauses, gestures, and tone. Once we reach a genuinely deep exchange, I do not want to interrupt its rhythm.

## 7. Sixteen-times Dream of the Red Chamber

At school, I heard the novel discussed partly as exam material: did you know the characters and the relevant events? Some students were advised to use the television adaptation if they could not get through the book; I also heard of people speeding it up dramatically, even to 16×. I understand the logic. If the exam mainly rewards knowing what happened, speeding through the plot may be efficient for that narrow goal.

But it is not the same as experiencing the work. A pause, an actor’s expression, the atmosphere of a scene, and the gradual change in a character may contribute little to a plot question while constituting much of what makes the scene worth watching. Knowing what happens is not the same as experiencing how it happens. The right speed depends on what I came for.

## 8. Leaving room for a thought of my own

Sometimes my phone dies or I leave it behind, and I walk across campus without listening to anything. At first I just notice the quiet. Then I realize my mind has not stopped working. A question comes back; one thought leads to another; I notice the trees, buildings, and people moving around me.

I later came across the term default mode network (DMN), associated with internally directed thought, including recalling personal experiences and imagining the future. I find it a useful way to think about what happens during these quiet stretches. When I am no longer chasing new information, my mind can revisit memories, feelings, and half-formed ideas, sometimes connecting things that had seemed unrelated. That is why I want to keep some time in which I am not listening, reading, or trying to finish anything. It does not have to produce an insight. Sometimes I relax; sometimes I work something out; sometimes nothing in particular happens. That is fine.
