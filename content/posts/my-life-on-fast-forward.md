body_zh: |
  我对播放器上的数字有些挑剔。视频常调到三倍，音频则多在2.1到2.25倍之间。能以0.05倍为单位微调，当然比几个相隔很远的选项更好。有时，恰恰是2.15倍，听起来最舒服。
  
  图书馆里、吃饭时、独自走路、取快递或做简单家务的时候，我都会听点什么。得到、B站、播客和课程回放，填进了不少日常空隙。除非正在和人说话，或刻意让头脑休息，我通常总能找到一段值得打开的内容。
  
  但我的倍速生活，并不只是在播放器上加快人声。我也会用解读代替完整材料，用一天集中听一门原本分散数周的课，或者为一个想弄明白的问题寻找更短的路径。这些选择确实丰富了我的生活，也让我越来越难对一段没有安排的时间心安理得。
  
  ## 一｜我怎样开始习惯两倍以上的速度
  
  ### 阅读原本就让我掌握节奏
  
  我喜欢自学，远早于高倍速成为习惯。高中准备物理竞赛，主要靠自己读书；通勤回家时，偶尔听音频或英语听力，大约开到一点五倍。到了大一，仍然以阅读为主。部分基础数学课程此前已经学过，我更习惯自己安排进度。
  
  课程比较少的时候，我在微信读书上读了很多电子书。那时最熟悉的音频平台是喜马拉雅，也试过从头听完整本书，却常觉得不如自己读来得合适。
  
  文字摊在眼前，我可以略过熟悉的论述，回到前面核对一个概念，在难句旁停一停，查点资料，或者干脆换一本书。走路时听书，我往往只能顺着一句接一句往下听。播放器当然有暂停和回退，但眼睛和双手正忙着别的事情，频繁操作就没那么方便。论证太长，容易丢掉上下文；内容太浅，又只能等讲述者慢慢说完。
  
  我在意的是，能否把时间留在自己真正需要的地方。阅读原本就给了我这种自由。
  
  ### 合适的内容，改变了听书的吸引力
  
  后来，书读得多了，也有些疲倦。我陆续接触到得到、樊登讲书，以及主题更集中、表达更口语化的课程。不必从头听完整本书，也能先进入一个陌生领域。找到适合这样听的内容之后，两倍以上的速度才逐渐变得顺手。
  
  大二上前后，课程量增加，需要学习的新内容也更多了。回放让我能够自己决定在何时、何地听课。在安静的图书馆，戴上耳机，把内容放到眼前的小屏幕上，我往往比坐在教室里更自在。遇到不懂的地方可以停，熟悉的内容可以快些过，也不用担心自己停下思考时，老师已经讲到下一页。
  
  改变并不是从一个新按钮开始的。按钮早就有了；变化的是内容、处境，以及我能支配自己时间的程度。
  
  ### 大学给了我更多空档
  
  高中时手机使用受限，课程组织着一天的节奏，吃饭也常和同学聊天。到了大学，越来越多时间需要自己安排。一个人吃饭，或在楼与楼之间走一段路，都可以顺手打开一节课。
  
  没有谁郑重向我推荐倍速。我只是看到了这个选项，试着用，然后一直用了下去。但自主安排时间的自由，也伴着一种渐强的要求：既然时间归我，就该把它用好。一段能够放进课程的空档，慢慢变成了我觉得应该放进课程的空档。
  
  ## 二｜2.15倍速里，我究竟在做什么
  
  ### 找到还能思考的速度
  
  太慢，我会无聊；太快，可能每个字都听见了，意思却来不及连起来。舒服的速度处在两者之间：注意力被带动着，脑子又没有被声音追赶。
  
  这是我的使用感受，不是经实验测出的最优倍率。它随讲者、内容密度和已有知识而变化。对我熟悉的人文社科讲解、访谈和对谈，我更愿意加速；但一段难懂的哲学论证，不会因为属于文科就变得容易。一张电路图，也可能要看上几分钟，尽管讲解它只用了三十秒。
  
  有时某个道理忽然连通，我希望留住那十几秒、甚至近一分钟的兴奋，顺着它再想一点。前提是我真的理解了。倘若在将懂未懂的地方匆匆滑过去，就连恍然大悟的机会也一起跳过了。
  
  ### 有些十几秒，我一定倒回去
  
  我不会为每个漏听的细节都回放。重新找准一句话的位置，有时比听那句话还费时间。对泛听播客里一个无关紧要的小点，我可能就继续往下听了。
  
  但笑声会改变这个判断。课堂上忽然笑成一片，我却没听清老师刚才说了什么，这时我一定会倒回十几秒，调回原速，甚至降到0.75倍。我要听到那个笑话、那句妙语，或是引起大家反应的互动。
  
  所以我并非总在追求最短播放时间。我不断判断，哪些几秒值得多留一点注意力。这个办法也有一个弱点：在决定停不停之前，我得先认出它的价值。
  
  ### 第二遍，才是真正需要付出的功夫
  
  环境经济学给过我一个提醒。当时加速听课，觉得大致明白；几天后再看课件，有些内容却已经讲不清。考前回去复习，还是得重新把它们理顺。
  
  第二遍比从零开始容易些，结构和概念毕竟有了印象。但熟悉不等于掌握。仅凭这一次经历，我也不能断言，没弄明白究竟是因为倍速，还是内容本身难、练习不够。
  
  真正需要学扎实的课程，我会做题、看课件、找文字资料、向AI提问，也会自己推导。有时索性不听了，直接读。推导尤其容易被我跳过：被动跟着听，大脑负担很重；后来肯花力气亲手做一遍，才发现最有帮助的恰恰是这一部分。
  
  科研中也会有不再计算的时刻。一个电路问题，或一段经济学论证，把我吸引进去，我就愿意一直想下去，不再数今天翻了几页、碰了几道题。我只想弄懂眼前这个问题。这样的功夫，没有可以代替它的播放倍率。
  
  ## 三｜先浓缩内容，再加快播放
  
  ### 十小时、三十分钟、十分钟
  
  一段讲书，在我碰到倍速按钮之前，就已经替我加速了一次。讲述者挑出主线，重新组织材料，把最有力量的例子放到显眼的位置。我再把这份经过编辑的内容调快。
  
  假设一本完整有声书要听十小时，讲书把它浓缩成三十分钟，三倍速再把三十分钟变成十分钟。这是两种不同的操作：前一次删减和重组内容，后一次加快剩余内容的到达。收听时间确实大幅缩短，却不意味着十小时的理解可以原封不动地装进十分钟。
  
  我所说的倍速生活，也包括这种广义的加速。我希望先用一个经过选择的入口，判断什么值得更长的投入。有时一天听过十本左右的解读，再挑一两本找原书；另一些只留下几个念头，甚至没留下多少记忆。即便如此，知道世界上还有这么一个问题、自己也许会喜欢它，仍然有价值。
  
  ### 浓缩的力量，在于把我带到精彩之处
  
  我记得一段讲述，先讲一个带病也坚持参加勒班陀海战的年轻士兵，后来才揭出他的名字：塞万提斯。我原先知道《堂吉诃德》的作者，这一刻却像是看见了一个人怎样为自己的信念冒险。理想主义忽然不再只是文学评价。带病参战有传记材料支持；那个形象给我的震动，则属于我自己的感受。 [1]
  
  还有一段讲书，把史汀生1926年到访京都，与他后来反对把京都列为原子弹目标联系起来。庞大的历史决策，被放回一座曾经亲自走过的城市。我被“一段寻常经历可能在多年后发生作用”这个念头打动。不过，史料能够确认到访和干预，不能据此认定，仅仅一次旅行就救下了京都。 [2]
  
  这些故事让人难忘，不是因为我把声音调快了，而是因为内容本身，以及讲述者的组织。倍速让我在同样的时间里接触更多解读，好的解读则让我更快抵达有力量的部分。我珍惜这种浓缩，不必再替它附加一个“加速能增强记忆”的结论。
  
  看泽连斯基的一次访谈，也留下过不同的印象。具体说了哪些话，我未必还能复述，但他说起战争、国家和人民时，我感到的那种情绪强度还在。被一个人的表达触动，并不意味着赞同他的所有立场。有时留在记忆里的，不是一个命题，而是说话者的处境。
  
  ### 原书不是高潮的合集
  
  听过《克里米亚战争史》的浓缩解读，再去读书，我才发现，里面有那么多等待、消耗和外交博弈。精彩的瞬间当然存在，但它们之间还有很长的路。
  
  接连听很多讲书，容易让我对书产生一种玫瑰色的想象：好书似乎就该不断给人启发，不断出现值得记住的片段。读到原书觉得慢时，我才意识到，自己是在拿完整的叙述，和别人筛过一遍的精彩部分比较。
  
  这很像一个普通的日子，与后来挑出来代表它的几张照片。筛选让内容更容易进入，也会悄悄改变我对原貌的期待。倘若只习惯高光时刻，许多值得读的书，乃至普通生活本身，都会显得不够好看。
  
  ## 四｜趁兴趣还在，走进一个世界
  
  ### 那门不想放下的课
  
  孙乃树的《西方美术欣赏》，是我惦记很久的一门课。真正开始听，一幅画牵出下一幅画，一个问题又引出另一个。我从早上听到晚上，第二天醒来，还是想先接着听。
  
  它有讲故事的吸引力：每讲完一段，总还有些什么留在后面。我并不是强迫自己划掉一项任务，而是想留在那个刚被打开的世界里。
  
  这是集中听课给我的实际好处。对一个主题兴趣正浓时，我可能想在一两天内听过十五小时的课程，而不愿把它拖成数周。有些上午听完三四节长课，满足感很强。节约时间只是其中一部分；另一部分，是好奇心已经出发，不必再等课表慢慢跟上。
  
  当然，兴趣也不会一直那么浓。听完美术课，我一度想把相关艺术课程都学一遍。后来继续探索了一部分，最初的兴奋渐渐淡下来。这不意味着兴趣虚假，只意味着不能把一个下午的热情，当成对整个领域的长期承诺。
  
  ### 我不想离开的罗马
  
  陈文捷的《西方古代建筑艺术史》，尤其讲到罗马诞生的那一节，带给我的却是另一种感受。 [3]
  
  真正触动我的是历史想象的尺度。我仿佛置身城市上空，看那些建筑沉积着不同时代的痕迹。建筑连着文明的起源、延绵的文脉，也连着无数曾在其间生活的人。我希望图片停得久一点，让自己顺着讲述，把这些联系一点点想出来。
  
  这时候，我完全不想加速。我想在一句话里多待片刻，回看一幅图，让脑海中的城市更清晰。听完之后并没有另一个成果等着领取；此刻能够在想象中抵达那里，已经是我想要的。
  
  下面这幅画适合放在这里：雅克－路易·大卫1799年创作的《萨宾妇女》。我把它选作一幅愿意反复停留的图像，而不是用它证明课程当时展示了什么。 [4]

  电影、戏剧、音乐会也会给我这样的时刻。一个停顿、一道目光、一句旋律，并没有耽搁下一条信息；它本身就是我想经历的部分。到了这样的地方，我会回看，甚至希望它迟一些结束。
  
  ## 五｜省下的时间，去了哪里
  
  ### 听课便宜了，想听的也更多了
  
  一段三小时的内容，一小时就听完，我很少把另外两小时空着。通常会再开一门课。能够听得更多，反过来让更多内容进入“值得听”的范围。
  
  这让我想到杰文斯讨论燃料效率时提出的机制：一种用途所需的资源减少，可能带来更大的需求，抵消原先的节省。我的听课只是一个类比，不能当成对同一现象的测量。但那个方向很熟悉：每门课花的时间少了，总听课时间反而增加。 [5]
  
  其中不少取舍，是我有意识作出的。我本来就喜欢学习，发现一个陌生领域，也是真实的收获。某件事变得更值得做，于是愿意多做一些，不能直接等同于失去自制力。
  
  难处在于，我挤掉了什么。下一门课有明确的标题和时长；一段散步、一场没有计划的闲聊、一小时安静，却很少附带同样清楚的回报说明。把它们放在一起比较，课程有时还没等另一边开口，就已经胜出了。
  
  ### 进度条究竟记了什么
  
  对探索性的泛听，我常用内容数量和原始时长衡量进度。一个上午听过几节长课，而且大致跟得上，就会觉得这半天过得很充实。
  
  我知道它们只是替代指标。正式课程可以看成绩、看作业和解题；播客没有期末考试，我也许只想获得一个入口、一个问题，或一个日后能与别的东西相连的念头。为每个偶然听到的知识点专门安排检验，反而违背了泛听的目的。
  
  但指标也有自己的吸引力。听完的数量看得见，判断力的变化看不见。我可能因为一个数字增长而满意，却还没有问自己，理解究竟发生了什么变化。感觉高效与真正有效有重合，却不是同一回事。
  
  ### 收藏夹里，住着许多未来的自己
  
  刚开始在B站找课时，我的收藏夹增长得很快。微信读书的书架也是如此。收藏令人愉快：那一刻，我已经能想象学完之后的自己。
  
  真正开始学，却可能要经过很长一段笨拙的、不理解的过程。我更愿意打开那些很快就有收获的内容，而对需要长期吃苦才能见效的课程犹豫。有时我想要的是学完的结果，还没有那么愿意去学眼前这门课。
  
  AI和更好的工具又让清单变长。既然事情容易了，为什么不多做一些？于是做法渐渐赶不上想法。以前参加竞赛，看到别人进度快，我就会紧张；如今，比较甚至可以由自己完成。旁边不必坐着谁，我也会想象，另一个人正在把同样的一小时用得更好。
  
  我想在通识上见多识广，也想在专业上钻得足够深：跨过学科界限时，有东西可以彼此连接；碰到困难问题时，能追问到答案背后的根据。这两种愿望都是真的，却不会因为都叫“学习”，就需要同一种时间安排。
  
  ### 工具也有入场费
  
  我曾花很久，想给教学平台的课程回放加上字幕。只听声音，似乎很快就会碰到理解的上限；有了文字，也许能跟上更高的速度。最后没有达到预期，我怀疑是平台限制，但没有确认原因。
  
  我并不把所有失败尝试都看成浪费。有些这样找到的工具，直到现在仍然有用。一项尝试可以在未来许多次使用中收回成本。但这毕竟是一笔回报不确定的投资，配置、试错和寻找工具的时间，都应该算进去。播放器快了一点，不会让那些已经花掉的小时消失。
  
  ## 六｜当效率开始要求别人
  
  ### 语速快，不等于表达简洁
  
  从中学起，就有人说我讲话太快。这早于我大量使用高倍速，不能把原因全推给一个软件。但两件事里都有同一种愿望：尽量在短时间里装进更多内容。
  
  我曾觉得，快速而清楚地表达，是替别人节约时间。可有时我说了一大段，对方的第一句回应却是：“你刚才说什么？”如果还得从头再来，双方的时间都没有省下。
  
  家人提醒我慢一点，我也试过。只是刻意放慢会让我不舒服，讲着讲着又快起来。难处不在于不知道怎么做，而在于急着把一个想法说出来时，还能记得照顾听的人。
  
  ### 那句我已经知道的话
  
  语音里，妈妈有时刚开了个头，我就觉得后面几句话已经猜到了。我会不耐烦，打断她，催她换个话题，或者说这些我都知道。
  
  挂断之后，常常又觉得自己本可以温和一点。父母花了很多年把我养大，我如今在北京，一学期回家一两次，却还在把几句熟悉的叮嘱，当成一场效率不高的信息传递。
  
  后悔并不意味着已经改变。明白这些，下次通话仍然可能不耐烦。有时我会事后和妈妈说，刚才不该打断，或者我们再多聊一会儿。我希望这点理解能真正落在通话里，而不只落在事后的反思里。
  
  我曾问AI，人最想重新经历怎样的时刻。原以为是初吻、孩子出生，或事业成功；留在我心里的，却是一个寻常的下午，和家人在一起。那是AI给出的回答，不是调查排名。它打动我，是因为我能想象那个画面，也能想象自己在它仍然存在的时候，怎样匆匆把它过掉。
  
  ### 聊天不是把简历读一遍
  
  我不喜欢把一个人当成简历来读。我想听的，是只有他能带来的东西：我没有经历过的事，预料之外的看法，以及一个人的不同经历怎样碰撞出独特的判断。
  
  认真对待一场谈话时，我会把手机放远，专心看着对方。表情、停顿、动作和语调，都值得留意。一旦进入真正的深谈，我不愿破坏它的节奏。惊喜也是其中的快乐：有那么一会儿，我写不出对方的下一句话。
  
  但一场谈话会走到哪里，不能总在开头判断。某句随口的建议，当时觉得寻常，后来却可能重新浮上来。我没有一个特别戏剧化的故事可讲，这更多是一种反复出现的感受，使我不敢把每一分钟单独标价。
  
  想让AI提炼会议里的重点时，也有类似的犹豫。为一个目的，知道结论或许就够了；为另一个目的，认识人、了解他们怎样想，或让谈话自行展开，就需要在场。我可以不喜欢空洞的客套，却不能因此认定，摘要之外都没有东西。
  
  ## 七｜十六倍速的《红楼梦》
  
  ### 对一道考题而言，这可能很合理
  
  高中时，我听到过把《红楼梦》当作考试材料的讨论：人物关系是否了解，某个情节是否知道，题目来了能不能答。记忆里，老师曾建议，读不下原著的同学可以通过电视剧熟悉内容；也听说有人继续加速，甚至调到十六倍。
  
  我理解这种做法的吸引力。我自己也属于会考虑用改编作品了解情节的人。接下来那一步优化，更不难理解：如果考试主要奖励“知道发生了什么”，为什么要花那么长时间，看着它慢慢发生？
  
  对一个很窄的目标，这确实是一种高效策略。可它并不等于充分体验作品。回答之前的停顿，演员的神情，一场戏的气息，以及人物感受一点点发生的变化，对情节题可能帮助有限，却可能构成这场戏最值得看的部分。
  
  因此，在讨论倍率之前，先得知道自己来做什么。一个准备答题的人，和一个想进入作品的人，面对同一段录像，完全可能需要不同的速度。
  
  ### 假如五倍速也能完全听懂
  
  如果五倍速仍能保证理解，我会愿意在某些内容上尝试。我并不执着于某个数字。
  
  但得先问清楚，“理解”保留的是什么。事后能说出论点？能重新完成推导？还是有时间察觉一句话为什么打动自己？前一种没有损失，并不自动意味着后两种也被完整保留。
  
  这正是罗马那段体验对我的意义。我并没有因为不肯加速而未能高效完成一门课。我当时做的事情，本来就包括花时间置身其中。承认这一点之后，就不必再把每一次主动停留都称作对效率的让步。
  
  ## 八｜给自己的想法留一点空地
  
  ### 一段没有输入的路
  
  有时手机没电，或者忘了带，原本会被音频填满的一段路就安静了。我开始看校园里的树，看经过的人，看他们各自忙着自己的事情。某个带了很久的问题，也可能以没那么用力的方式重新出现。
  
  旅行时更容易发生这种变化。离开熟悉的生活安排，我不再那么急着把每个空档装满。回到上海家里，也会松下来；但都市的节奏又容易把人带回紧迫之中。我的速度不只藏在播放器里，也藏在每天经过的环境里。
  
  这些路不需要每次都带来重大顿悟。但我确实感觉到，不断输入的时候，自己的念头很少有一段不被打断的展开机会。
  
  ### 让思绪自己走一会儿
  
  后来我才知道，这种让思绪自由游走的状态，和人们所说的默认模式网络（Default Mode Network，DMN）有些关联。它常被用来描述人在没有紧盯外部任务时，回忆往事、想象未来、让念头彼此联结的一类脑活动。
  
  这个概念让我想到，散步时的空白并不是大脑停工；它可能正是在整理已有的记忆和感受，让原本不相干的东西慢慢连起来。对我来说，散步和短暂放空，是给这些联系一点自然形成的空间。
  
  ### 十分钟，不需要交出成果
  
  我希望每天留十分钟，或者走一段不赶时间的路，不听、不读，也不处理别的任务。有时思考，有时看看周围，有时只是累了。并不要求自己把大脑清空。
  
  连这个愿望，也暴露了我难以摆脱的习惯：我最先为停下来找到的理由，是它可能带来更好的想法。也许确实如此。但若每次休息都必须交出一个灵感，我不过是又给休息添了一项考核。
  
  我仍然想努力工作，广泛学习，做成一些不容易的事，也没有打算戒掉高倍速。我想保留的是选择它的判断力，而不要求生活中的其他部分，都用同一把尺子证明自己值得。
  
  有些课，我想早点听完。有些声音，我还想多听一会儿。我希望能在仍然听得见的时候，分清这两件事。

I am particular about the numbers on a media player. Video often runs at 3×; audio usually somewhere between 2.1× and 2.25×. Given the choice, I would rather adjust the speed in steps of 0.05 than choose between a few large jumps. Sometimes 2.15× is the setting that feels right.

I listen in the library, over meals, on walks, while collecting a delivery or doing simple chores. Dedao, Bilibili, podcasts and recorded courses supply much of the material. Unless I am speaking with someone or deliberately leaving my mind alone, I usually find something to put in the gap.

My life on fast-forward involves more than accelerating a voice. I also choose summaries over full-length material, take a course in one concentrated stretch, and look for a shorter route to whatever I want to understand. Some of these choices have made my life substantially richer. Others have made it harder to leave any part of the day unaccounted for.

## 1. How I moved beyond 2×

### Reading gave me control

I liked self-study well before high-speed playback became a habit. In high school, I prepared for physics competitions mainly through books. On the journey home, I sometimes listened to audio or English listening material at around 1.5×. During my first year at university, I still relied heavily on reading; I had already studied some of the foundational mathematics, and preferred setting my own pace.

When my course load was lighter, I spent much of the spare time reading ebooks on WeRead. The audio platform I knew best was Ximalaya, where I tried listening to entire books. For my purposes, this often worked less well than reading them.

With a book open, I can skim a familiar passage, return to an earlier argument, stop at a difficult sentence, look something up, or decide that another book would be a better use of my time. While listening on the move, I mostly follow one sentence after another. Audio players have pause and rewind buttons, but using them repeatedly is more cumbersome when my eyes and hands are occupied. A long argument can lose its thread; an easy passage can take far longer than it needs to.

What I wanted was control over where to spend time. Reading had already given me some of that control.

### Better material changed the calculation

Later, sustained reading began to feel tiring, and I found other ways of listening: Dedao, Fan Deng's book talks, more focused lectures and recorded courses. A short, conversational explanation could introduce an unfamiliar subject without asking me to follow an entire book from beginning to end. Once I found material that suited this kind of listening, speeds above 2× became much more attractive.

Around the start of my second year, coursework also became heavier and less familiar. Recordings let me study at a time and place I chose. In a quiet library, with headphones and the screen close in front of me, I often felt more comfortable and less distracted than in a classroom. I could pause without falling behind the lecturer, or move quickly through something I already knew.

The button was not new. The combination of content, circumstances and freedom was.

### University supplied the gaps

At school, phone use was restricted, classes organized much of the day, and I talked with classmates more often during meals. University gave me more time to arrange for myself. A meal alone or a walk between buildings could become another listening session.

Nobody had to persuade me. I had noticed the speed setting, tried it, and kept using it. But the freedom to organize my day came with a growing expectation that I should use that freedom well. An interval that could hold a lecture began to feel like an interval that ought to hold one.

## 2. What happens at 2.15×

### A pace I can think at

Too slow, and I get bored. Too fast, and I can still hear the words without keeping up with the thought. The useful range lies between the two. At the right speed, I can remain alert and follow an argument without feeling chased by it.

This is a description of my experience, not a scientifically established optimum. The number changes with the speaker, the density of the material and what I already know. Familiar humanities lectures and conversational interviews often tolerate a higher speed for me. A difficult philosophical argument does not become easy because it belongs to the humanities. A circuit diagram may require several minutes even if its spoken explanation lasts thirty seconds.

When something clicks, I want enough time to enjoy and develop the connection. An insight needs to arrive before I can have an aha moment. Accelerating past the part I do not understand defeats the point.

### The ten seconds I will replay

I do not rewind every missed detail. Locating a sentence can take longer than hearing it, and for a minor point in a casual podcast I may decide to keep going.

Laughter changes that calculation. Sometimes a class erupts just after the lecturer says something I have missed. I will go back ten or fifteen seconds, then listen at normal speed—or even 0.75×—until I hear the line. I want to catch the joke, the surprising remark, the interaction that made the room respond.

I am not trying to finish every recording as quickly as possible. I am continually deciding which seconds deserve more of my attention. The weakness in this method is that I have to recognize their value before deciding whether to stop.

### The second pass is real work

Environmental economics gave me a useful warning. I listened quickly and felt that I understood. A few days later, some of the slides no longer made sense to me. When I returned to the material before the exam, I had to work through it again.

The second pass felt easier than starting from nothing. I recognized the structure and remembered having encountered the ideas. But familiarity had not been mastery. Nor could I tell from that experience alone whether the gaps came from speed, the difficulty of the material, or insufficient practice.

For courses I need to learn properly, I use problems, slides, written explanations, questions to AI and my own derivations. Sometimes I stop listening altogether and read. I have skipped derivations because following them passively felt demanding, only to discover later that working through them was the part that helped most.

In research, the calculation can disappear. A circuit question or an economic argument catches my attention, and I stay with it without counting how many pages or problems I have completed. I want to understand this one. There is no playback setting for that work.

## 3. Two kinds of compression

### Ten hours, thirty minutes, ten minutes

A book talk accelerates access before I touch the speed control. A narrator selects an argument, rearranges the material and gives the strongest examples more prominence. I then accelerate that already edited account.

Suppose a complete audiobook would take ten hours. A thirty-minute explanation reduces the material; playing that explanation at 3× reduces its duration to ten minutes. These are two different operations. The first leaves things out. The second makes what remains arrive faster. Together they reduce listening time enormously, but they do not deliver ten hours of understanding in ten minutes.

This is the broader sense in which I live on fast-forward. I often want a well-chosen introduction before deciding what deserves a longer commitment. On some days, I have listened to around ten book explanations and picked one or two books to pursue. Others have left only a few ideas, or little I can recall. Even then, a brief encounter can tell me that a subject exists and that I might care about it.

### Why the concentrated version can be powerful

One account began with a sick young soldier who insisted on fighting at Lepanto. Only later did it give his name: Miguel de Cervantes. Knowing the author of Don Quixote and then encountering him as a person taking a risk for his convictions made idealism feel less like a literary label. Cervantes's participation despite illness is recorded in biographical material; the interpretation that moved me was my own. [1]

Another account connected Henry Stimson's visit to Kyoto in 1926 with his later opposition to its use as an atomic-bomb target. The narrative brought a vast historical decision down to the scale of a remembered city. I was moved by the possibility that an ordinary visit could matter years later. The historical record does establish the visit and his intervention, but not the neat claim that a holiday alone saved Kyoto. [2]

The stories were not memorable because I played them quickly. They were memorable because of what they revealed and how they were told. Speed let me encounter more such explanations; the narrator's selection brought their strongest moments within reach. That distinction matters. I value the concentration without needing to claim that acceleration improves memory.

An interview with Volodymyr Zelensky left a different kind of impression. I no longer remember every sentence, but I remember the emotional force I felt as he spoke about war, his country and its people. Being affected by a speaker does not require endorsing all his positions. Sometimes what survives a recording is less a proposition than a sense of the person speaking.

### A book is not a collection of climaxes

After hearing a condensed account of The Crimean War , I read the book and found much more waiting, attrition and diplomacy than the concentrated version had prepared me for. The memorable scenes were there, but so were the long stretches between them.

A run of compelling summaries can make books seem as though they should consist entirely of revelations. Then the original feels slow. I had to notice what was happening: I was comparing an extended account with someone else's selection of its most gripping moments.

It resembles the difference between an ordinary day and the photographs chosen to represent it. Selection can make something easier to enter. It can also change my expectations of what the unedited experience should feel like. If I learn to tolerate only the highlights, I will find a great deal of worthwhile reading—and ordinary life—unreasonably slow.

## 4. Following an interest until it runs out

### The course I did not want to put down

Sun Naishu's Western Art Appreciation was a course I had wanted to hear for a long time. Once I began, one painting led to another, one question opened the next. I listened from morning into the evening. The following morning, I wanted to continue before doing anything else.

The sequence had the pull of a story: each installment left something unresolved. I was not forcing myself through a checklist. I wanted to stay inside the subject while it had that hold on me.

This is one of the real benefits of concentrated listening. When a subject captures my attention, I may want to cover a fifteen-hour course over a day or two rather than stretch it across weeks. On some mornings, completing three or four long class sessions feels intensely satisfying. The benefit is not merely time saved; it is being able to follow a live interest without waiting for the timetable to catch up.

The interest does not last forever. After the art course, I wanted to learn much more about painting and art history. I pursued some of it, then the initial intensity faded. That does not make the interest false. It does mean I should not mistake one enthusiastic afternoon for a permanent commitment to an entire field.

### The Rome I did not want to leave

Chen Wenjie's History of Western Ancient Architectural Art gave me a different experience, especially the lecture introducing Rome. [3]

What moved me was the scale of the historical imagination. I could picture myself above the city, looking at buildings that held traces of different ages. The lecture connected architecture with the beginnings of a civilization, its cultural memory and the lives that had accumulated around it. I wanted the pictures to remain on the screen while I followed those connections.

Here, I wanted the opposite of acceleration. I wanted to stay with a sentence, look again at an image, and allow the imagined city to become more vivid. There was no separate achievement waiting at the end of the listening. Being there in thought was already what I had come for.

The painting below belongs beside that memory: Jacques-Louis David's The Intervention of the Sabine Women , painted in 1799. I chose it as an image I want to spend time looking at, not as a record of what appeared in the lecture. [4]

A film, a play or a concert can make me feel the same way. A pause, a look or a musical phrase is not simply delaying the next piece of information. It is the part I am there to experience. When a moment has that quality, I may replay it rather than move on.

## 5. Where the saved time goes

### Cheaper listening, greater demand

When I finish a three-hour recording in an hour, I seldom leave the other two hours empty. I open another course. The ability to cover more material makes more material worth considering.

This reminds me of the mechanism behind Jevons's discussion of fuel efficiency: reducing the resources required for a use can expand demand enough to offset the saving. My listening is an analogy, not a measurement of the same phenomenon. But the pattern feels familiar: time per lecture falls while my total time spent listening rises. [5]

I make much of this trade deliberately. I like learning, and discovering an unfamiliar field is a real gain. Spending more time on something because it has become more rewarding is not automatically a failure of self-control.

The harder question is what gets displaced. A further lecture has an obvious title and duration. A walk, an unplanned conversation or an hour of quiet does not arrive with a similarly legible return. When I compare them, the lecture can win before I have given the other option a fair hearing.

### What my progress meter measures

For exploratory listening, I often use the quantity of material and its original duration as rough measures of progress. If I get through several long sessions in a morning and feel able to follow them, the morning feels substantial.

I know these are proxies. A test or a difficult assignment can tell me something about a formal course. A podcast has no such exam. I may want only an introduction, a useful question or an idea that will connect with something else later. Testing every incidental point would defeat that purpose.

Still, the proxy has a pull of its own. An increase in completed material is visible; a change in judgment is not. I can become satisfied with a number even when I have not asked what changed in my understanding. Feeling efficient and being effective overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

### My collection of possible selves

When I began exploring Bilibili courses, my saved list grew quickly. The same happened with the WeRead bookshelf. Saving a course is pleasant: I can already imagine the knowledge I will have after finishing it.

Actually learning it may involve a long, awkward stretch of not understanding. I am more willing to start material that offers immediate rewards than material whose value comes after sustained difficulty. Sometimes I want the result of studying more than I want to study that particular subject.

AI and better tools enlarge the list further. If a task has become easier, why not add it? My ideas for what I could do begin to outpace what I actually do. During my competition years, another student's faster progress could make me anxious; now I can supply the comparison myself. Nobody needs to be sitting beside me. I imagine that somebody else is using the same hour better.

I want breadth in my general education and depth in my work. I would like to know enough across fields to make unexpected connections, and to stay with a technical question until I understand what supports the answer. These are both serious ambitions. They do not become the same task because both can be described as learning.

### Tools have an entry fee

I once spent a long time trying to add subtitles to a university course recording. Speech alone seemed to impose a limit; perhaps text would help me follow at a higher speed. I did not get the setup working as intended. I suspected a platform restriction, but never established the cause.

I do not regard every failed attempt as wasted time. Some tools I have found this way remain useful. An experiment can pay off over many later uses. But that is an uncertain investment, and its setup cost belongs in the calculation. A faster player does not make the hours spent configuring it disappear.

## 6. When efficiency becomes a rule for other people

### Fast speech is not always concise speech

People have told me since school that I speak too quickly. That predates my heavy use of playback speed; I cannot honestly blame the habit on an app. But the desire to fit more into less time is familiar in both places.

I used to think of rapid, clear expression as a courtesy. Then I would finish explaining something and hear: “What did you just say?” If the listener needs the whole explanation again, neither of us has saved time.

My family has asked me to slow down. I try, feel uncomfortable with the slower pace, and gradually speed up again. The difficulty is not knowing what would help. It is remembering to do it while I am eager to get the thought out.

### The sentence I already know

On a voice call, my mother sometimes begins a sentence whose next few lines I feel I can predict. I become impatient. I interrupt, ask her to move on, or say that I already know.

Afterwards, I often wish I had been gentler. My parents spent years raising me. I live in Beijing now and return home only once or twice a term. Yet I can treat a few familiar reminders as if they were an inefficient delivery of information.

The regret is not proof that I have changed. I can recognize all this, call again, and still become impatient. Sometimes I tell my mother later that I should not have interrupted, or suggest that we talk a little more. I want the recognition to show up in the conversation, not only in my account of it afterwards.

Once I asked AI what moment people might most want to live again. I expected a milestone: a first kiss, the birth of a child, a major success. The answer that stayed with me was an ordinary afternoon with family. That was an AI response, not a survey result. It moved me because I could imagine the scene—and how easily I might hurry through it while it was still available.

### A conversation is not a résumé

I do not enjoy conversations that feel like someone reading out a résumé. I want the things only this person can bring: an experience I have not had, a view I could not have predicted, the way different parts of a life have produced an unusual judgment.

In conversations I care about, I put my phone away and pay attention to the person. Expressions, pauses, gestures and tone matter. Once we get into a genuinely deep exchange, I do not want to disturb its rhythm. Surprise is part of the pleasure: for a while, I cannot write the other person's next sentence for them.

But I cannot reliably know in advance where a conversation will go. A casual remark that seems unimportant at the time may later return as a useful suggestion. I do not have a neat, dramatic example to offer; it is a recurring impression that makes me wary of pricing every minute separately.

The same reservation applies when I want AI to summarize a meeting's preliminaries. Extracting decisions may be enough for one purpose. Meeting people, learning how they think or letting a conversation develop may require being there. I can dislike empty formalities without assuming that everything outside the summary was empty.

## 7. Sixteen-times Dream of the Red Chamber

### An efficient answer to an exam question

At school, I heard Dream of the Red Chamber discussed partly as examination material: did you know the characters and the relevant events? I remember a teacher suggesting the television adaptation as one route for students who struggled to get through the novel. I also remember hearing about students accelerating it drastically—even to 16×.

I understand the attraction. I too am inclined to consider an adaptation when the immediate task is to become familiar with a plot. The next optimization is easy to see: if the exam rewards recognizing what happened, why spend more time than necessary watching it happen?

This is an efficient response to a narrow objective. It does not follow that it is an adequate way to experience the work. The pause before a reply, an actor's expression, the atmosphere of a scene and the gradual change in how a character feels may contribute little to a plot question while constituting much of what makes the scene worth watching.

The target matters before the speed does. A person trying to answer an exam question and a person trying to inhabit a novel can use the same recording and rationally want different things from it.

### What would 5× leave out?

If 5× preserved my understanding perfectly, I would be tempted to use it for some material. I am not attached to a number for its own sake.

But I would need to ask what “understanding” meant in that promise. Could I identify the argument afterwards? Could I reproduce a derivation? Would I have time to notice why a sentence moved me? A claim that preserved one of these would not automatically preserve the others.

That is why my experience of Rome matters to me. I was not failing to finish a lecture efficiently. I was doing something whose value included spending time with it. Once I admit that, I no longer have to describe every chosen delay as a sacrifice of productivity.

## 8. Leaving room for a thought of my own

### The walk without an input

Sometimes my phone runs out of power or I leave it behind. The walk I would normally fill with audio becomes quiet. I notice the trees on campus, the people passing, the different ways they seem occupied with their own lives. A question I have been carrying may return in a less forced form.

Travel makes the change easier. Away from my ordinary routines, I am less intent on filling every gap. At home in Shanghai I can relax too, though the pace of a city can pull me back toward urgency. My speed is not entirely contained in the player; it is also in the situations I move through.

I do not need a major insight to justify these walks. But I do notice that constant input gives my own thoughts fewer uninterrupted chances to develop.

### Letting the Mind Wander

I later learned that this kind of inward, wandering thought is often discussed in connection with the default mode network (DMN). The term is used for brain activity associated with things like remembering, imagining what might happen, and letting thoughts move between ideas when we are not focused on a particular task.

It gave me a useful way to think about those quiet walks: my mind is not necessarily idle just because I am not feeding it new information. It may be sorting through memories and feelings, allowing connections to form in their own time. For me, a walk or a few quiet minutes is a way to leave room for that process.

### Ten minutes with nothing to prove

I want to reserve a little time—perhaps ten minutes a day, or an unhurried walk—without audio, reading or another task. Sometimes I would think; sometimes I would notice my surroundings; sometimes I would simply be tired. I do not mean a requirement to make my mind perfectly blank.

Even this proposal reveals the habit I am trying to loosen. My first justification is that a pause might produce better ideas. That may be true. But if I demand a useful idea from every pause, I have merely given rest another performance target.

I still want to work hard, learn broadly and make difficult things possible. I do not intend to give up high-speed playback. I want to retain the judgment to use it without asking every other part of life to justify itself by the same measure.

There are lectures I want to finish sooner. There are voices I want to keep hearing. I am trying to become better at telling the difference while there is still time to listen.
